/lit-review, I choose you!

A literature review skill for Claude. Why you want your own, which tools it needs, and how to use it. 10 minutes.

Why your own skill

Without a skill

You explain your process again every session. Claude summarizes from memory, invents references, and every round looks different.

With a skill

Your process is written down once. Claude follows it every time: plan, search, add, read, log, synthesize. You only make the decisions.

Refining it makes it better. Every time you notice something that did not go as planned, you can edit the skill and avoid it next time.

Run a round→ Notice a gap→ Add one line→ Next round is better

Tools, and why

needed

A search engine

For example OpenAlex or Google Scholar. References come from a database, not from Claude's memory. It also finds who cites a paper and checks DOIs.

needed

A backed-up place for the review

For example git or Google Drive. Your reading log and review files live there: your judgment, safe and with history.

optional

A library

For example Zotero or Mendeley. Papers, PDFs and your highlights in one place, no paper added twice, citekeys for LaTeX.

The repo has two versions: lit-review uses OpenAlex and git. lit-review-zotero adds Zotero. Pick one.

How to use it

Get the ai-for-research repo. The first time, clone it. Later, pull for updates:

git clone https://github.com/Tonidor/ai-for-research.git
cd ai-for-research && git pull

Copy one of the two skills into the folder where you do your literature review. Without Zotero:

cd <your review folder>
mkdir -p .claude/skills
cp -R <path to>/ai-for-research/literature-review/skills/lit-review .claude/skills/

With Zotero, copy skills/lit-review-zotero instead. It needs the Zotero setup from the README first.

Start Claude in that folder, the one that holds .claude/skills/, and type /lit-review (or /lit-review-zotero) and your question. A round has seven steps. Claude stops twice and waits for you.

  1. Check what is known. Claude greps your reading log.
  2. Plan. Question, sub-questions, search terms, what is in or out.
  3. Search and add. OpenAlex. Papers without an abstract need a reason to be added.
  4. Full texts. Claude fetches open access papers and lists the rest for you to download.
  5. Reading log. One entry per paper: problem, approach, finding, relevance, and a proposed tier.
  6. Must-reads. One to three papers for you, in reading order.
  7. Synthesis. Answers per sub-question, contradictions, gaps. Then commit.

A reading log entry looks like this:

### [smith2020] Short title
DOI: 10.1234/abcd · Tier: core · Source: full PDF · 2026-10-05
- Problem: what gap the paper addresses.
- Approach: the setup, with the numbers a model needs.
- Finding: the result, with numbers.
- Relevance: what it means for your project.

Claude proposes the tier, and you can change it. Source says what the entry was written from, for example the full PDF, only the abstract, or only how other papers describe it. That tells you how far to trust it.

Try it

Pick a topic from your own research and run a short round:

/lit-review Do a short review round on YOUR TOPIC. Keep it to about 10 papers.

Replace YOUR TOPIC before you send it.

Watch where it stops for you, read the entries it writes, and note one thing you would change. That is your first edit to the skill.
